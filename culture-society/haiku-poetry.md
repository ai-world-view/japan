# Haiku & Poetry

**Category**: Culture & Society  
**Key figures**: Matsuo Bashō, Ueshima Onitsura, Kobayashi Issa, Yosa Buson, Masaoka Shiki

## Summary

Haiku is Japan's classical form of short poetry—a three-line verse of 5–7–5 syllables (in Japanese morae)—that distills moment, season, and spiritual insight into compressed language. Emerging from the linked-verse poetry tradition (*renga*) of the medieval period, haiku developed in the Edo period as an independent art form and became the dominant poetic practice of urban commoners and samurai alike. The form rose to its greatest heights in the 17th and 18th centuries through the work of Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694), considered haiku's supreme master, whose travel journal *Oku no Hosomichi* (*The Narrow Road to the Deep North*, 1694) combined haiku with prose narrative to create a hybrid literary masterpiece. Bashō's approach emphasized *haikai no ku*—the spirit of comic detachment and spiritual depth—and introduced the concept of *sabi* (loneliness, rusticity) and *karumi* (lightness, simplicity) into haiku aesthetics.

The classical period of haiku extended through the 18th century with poets such as Ueshima Onitsura (1661–1738), who pioneered a more serious philosophical approach, and Kobayashi Issa (1763–1827), whose warm, humorous verses often centered on humble subjects and creatures. Yosa Buson (1716–1783) brought *haiga*—haiku combined with ink painting—to new heights of artistic sophistication. The 19th century saw haiku's transition from a primarily Edo-period entertainment into a recognized literary art form, with the Meiji-era poet and critic Masaoka Shiki (1867–1902) championing haiku's modern revival through the journal *Hototogisu* and advocating a return to close observation of nature and direct experience, which he termed *shasei* (sketch from life).

Beyond haiku, classical Japanese poetry encompasses several major traditions: *tanka* (a five-line poem of 5–7–5–7–7 syllables, predating haiku by over a millennium); *waka* (the general term for Japanese verse as opposed to *kanshi*, or Chinese verse); the imperial *Kokinshū* (905) and other court-sponsored anthologies; and the refined aesthetics of *uta* (song) in Heian court culture. The integration of nature observation, seasonal reference (*kigo*), and philosophical depth forms the core of Japanese poetic tradition and continues to influence poetry worldwide.

## Origins: From Renga to Haiku

Haiku emerged from *haikai no renga* (playful linked verse), a collaborative poetic form in which two or more poets composed alternating stanzas. In the classical *renga* tradition, a sequence began with an opening stanza called the *hokku* (literally "starting verse")—a three-part unit of 5–7–5 morae that set the seasonal tone (*kigo*) and included a *kireji* ("cutting word") that divided the verse into contrasting images. By the 16th century, *haikai* masters such as Matsunaga Teitoku (1571–1653) had formalized haiku composition into a school-based tradition with strict conventions.

The decisive shift came in the mid-17th century when Matsuo Bashō and his circle began composing standalone *hokku* that could stand independently of their linked-verse context, transforming the opening verse into a complete, self-contained poem. This innovation, combined with Bashō's emphasis on spiritual depth and natural observation, separated haiku from the merely comic and established it as a serious literary form in its own right. Bashō's school (the *Shōmon*, or "Gate of Bashō") produced some of the tradition's most celebrated poems and established the aesthetic principles that would guide haiku for the next three centuries.

## Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694)

The undisputed master of haiku, Matsuo Bashō was born in Iga Province (modern Mie Prefecture) into a minor samurai family and initially served as a page to a local lord before dedicating himself entirely to poetry. Moving to Edo (modern Tokyo) around 1672, he quickly established himself as a prominent haiku teacher, gathering a circle of devoted students in his modest cottage in the Fukagawa district, known as the Bashō-an (Banana Tree Hermitage)—the banana tree (*bashō*) given to him by a student became his pen name.

Bashō undertook a series of poetic pilgrimages across Japan, most famously the journey of 1689 that became *Oku no Hosomichi* (*The Narrow Road to the Deep North*), a 2,400-kilometer trek through northern Honshu lasting approximately 156 days. The text, finalized in 1694 shortly before Bashō's death, interweaves haiku with reflective prose (*haibun*) and stands as one of the most celebrated works in all of Japanese literature. Among its most famous verses, the "old pond" poem (composed 1686) encapsulates core haiku aesthetics:

> *furuike ya*  
> *kawazu tobikomu*  
> *mizu no oto*

("An old silent pond / A frog jumps into the pond— / Splash! Silence again.")

This single poem demonstrates the juxtaposition of stillness and sudden motion, the seasonal reference (frogs appear in spring), and the evocation of *ma* (the pregnant pause of silence that follows the splash). Bashō's later aesthetic philosophy of *karumi* (lightness) emphasized naturalness and the poet's spiritual identification with the subject—what he called *makoto* (sincerity)—as the highest haiku virtue. He died in Osaka on November 28, 1694, surrounded by disciples, having composed an estimated 1,000 haiku across his career.

## The Four Great Haiku Masters

Japanese literary tradition identifies four poets as haiku's classical masters:

**Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694)**: The supreme master; established haiku's spiritual depth and aesthetic principles. Associated with *sabi* (austere loneliness), *wabi* (rustic simplicity), and *karumi* (lightness). His school (*Shōmon*) produced disciples including Mukai Kyorai and Nozawa Bonchō.

**Yosa Buson (1716–1783)**: Poet and painter, born in Settsu Province (modern Osaka). Buson revived Bashō's classical tradition in the mid-18th century after a period of artistic decline, and introduced the concept of *haiga*—haiku combined with expressive ink painting. Buson's poems are noted for their vivid visual imagery and painterly composition, strongly influenced by his work as a professional literati painter (*bunjin-ga*). He is credited with re-establishing the haiku tradition on a scholarly footing through his "Back to Bashō" movement of the 1760s–1770s.

**Kobayashi Issa (1763–1827)**: Born to a farming family in Shinano Province (modern Nagano), Issa endured repeated personal tragedies—the early deaths of his mother, stepmother, wife, and four children—that gave his poetry a distinctive warmth and compassion for small, vulnerable creatures. His most celebrated poem, written after the death of his infant daughter Sato in 1819, reads: *"Tsuyu no yo wa / tsuyu no yo nagara / sari nagara"* ("This world of dew / is only a world of dew— / and yet... and yet..."). Issa composed approximately 20,000 haiku over his lifetime, more than any other classical master.

**Masaoka Shiki (1867–1902)**: A Meiji-era poet, critic, and journalist from Matsuyama (modern Ehime Prefecture), Shiki died young from tuberculosis at age 34 but transformed haiku into a modern literary form. Writing in the newspaper *Nihon* from 1892 onward, he coined the term "haiku" to distinguish the standalone short poem from its linked-verse origins, championed *shasei* (sketching from life) as haiku's core methodology, and edited the influential journal *Hototogisu* (founded 1897), which became Japan's premier haiku publication. Shiki's disciples Kawahigashi Hekigotō and Takahama Kyoshi carried his legacy into the 20th century.

## Aesthetic Concepts

Classical haiku poetry is structured around several interrelated aesthetic principles:

**Kigo (seasonal reference)**: Every traditional haiku must contain a word or phrase indicating the season (*kigo*), chosen from a canonical reference (*saijiki* or haiku almanac). Seasonal references include natural phenomena, traditional events, and culturally significant plants and animals. Cherry blossoms (*sakura*) represent spring; the cuckoo (*hototogisu*) represents early summer; the harvest moon represents autumn; snow (*yuki*) represents winter.

**Kireji (cutting word)**: A grammatical or rhetorical marker that creates a juxtaposition or pause within the haiku, contrasting two separate images or moments. In Japanese, specific particles or verb endings serve this function (*ya*, *kana*, *keri*). The *kireji* is considered the most technically demanding element of haiku composition.

**Ma (negative space / pause)**: The evocative emptiness or silence between images, analogous to the negative space in Japanese ink painting and garden design. The best haiku achieve their power not through explicit statement but through what is left unsaid, allowing the reader to complete the image.

**Wabi-sabi**: The combined aesthetic of *wabi* (rustic simplicity, imperfection) and *sabi* (the beauty of age, solitude, and transience). Introduced into haiku aesthetics by Bashō, *wabi-sabi* values the understated, the worn, and the incomplete over polished perfection. A moss-covered stone, a thatched hermitage, or a faded autumn leaf exemplify *wabi-sabi* in haiku imagery.

**Karumi (lightness)**: Bashō's late-career aesthetic ideal, emphasizing naturalness, simplicity, and an almost effortless lightness of touch. In contrast to heavy philosophical verse, *karumi* haiku appear spontaneous and natural, like a stone skipping across water.

## The Broader Tanka and Waka Tradition

Haiku exists within a much older poetic tradition. *Tanka* (short poem), a five-line verse of 5–7–5–7–7 morae, predates haiku by over a thousand years and was the dominant poetic form of the Nara (710–794) and Heian (794–1185) courts. The *Man'yōshū* (Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves, c. 759), Japan's oldest extant anthology, contains over 4,500 *tanka* by poets including Kakinomoto no Hitomaro and Ōtomo no Yakamochi. The *Kokinshū* (Collection of Old and New Poems, 905), compiled at imperial command by Ki no Tsurayuki and others, established the formal conventions of classical *waka* for centuries and included Ki no Tsurayuki's famous *Kana* preface—the first extended critical essay written in Japanese rather than Chinese.

The Heian period produced two of Japan's most celebrated literary figures whose work intersected with poetry: [Murasaki Shikibu](../people/murasaki-shikibu.md), whose *Genji monogatari* (*The Tale of Genji*, c. 1010) includes hundreds of *tanka* exchanges and is considered the world's first psychological novel; and Sei Shōnagon, whose *Makura no Sōshi* (*The Pillow Book*, c. 1002) combined prose observations with poetic sensibility. The later *Shin Kokinshū* (New Collection of Old and New Poems, 1205), compiled under the direction of the cloistered Emperor Go-Toba, represents the apex of classical *waka* with an aesthetic of complex allusive variation (*honkadori*) associated with Fujiwara no Teika (1162–1241).

## International Influence

Haiku began reaching Western audiences in the 1870s–1890s through translations by scholars including Basil Hall Chamberlain, whose essay "Bashō and the Japanese Poetical Epigram" (1902) introduced the form to English readers. By the early 20th century, haiku had a profound impact on Anglophone modernist poetry, particularly the Imagist movement led by Ezra Pound and Amy Lowell. Pound's 1913 poem "In a Station of the Metro"—"The apparition of these faces in the crowd; / Petals on a wet, black bough"—directly embodies haiku's two-image juxtaposition and compressed observation.

The mid-20th century Beat Generation writers, particularly Jack Kerouac, Gary Snyder, and Allen Ginsberg, drew extensively on haiku aesthetics. Kerouac composed thousands of haiku and wrote an influential essay on the form; Snyder, who studied classical Japanese and Zen Buddhism, became one of the most sophisticated English-language haiku practitioners. Today, the Haiku Society of America (founded 1968) and parallel organizations in Europe, Australia, and throughout the Americas promote haiku composition and scholarship, and the annual Haiku Foundation Touchstone Awards recognize excellence in English-language haiku.

## Significance

Haiku represents one of Japan's most distinctive cultural contributions to world literature and aesthetics. Its economy of language—achieving profound meaning in just seventeen syllables—has fascinated Western poets and artists since the late 19th century, influencing modernist movements including Imagism and the Beat poets. The form embodies core Japanese aesthetic values: the acceptance of impermanence and incompleteness (*wabi-sabi*), the emphasis on suggestion over statement, and the close attentiveness to nature and seasonal cycles that pervades Japanese culture. Matsuo Bashō's mastery elevated haiku from a popular entertainment to a spiritual discipline and art form worthy of lifelong study. In contemporary Japan, haiku composition remains an active cultural practice across all social classes, with amateur poetry circles (*haiku kai*) and annual national competitions sustaining the tradition. The form's influence on Japanese visual arts, particularly in ink painting (*sumi-e*) and garden design, underscores its centrality to Japanese aesthetic philosophy.
