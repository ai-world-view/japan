# Nara Period

The **Nara Period** (710–794) was Japan's first permanent imperial capital era, characterized by the establishment of a Chinese-style bureaucratic state, the flourishing of Buddhist institutional power, and the cultural synthesis of Tang influences with indigenous Japanese traditions. Named after the city of *Heijō-kyō* (奈良, modern Nara), this seventy-four-year span witnessed the codification of Japan's state administration, the construction of monumental Buddhist temples, and the literary emergence of the *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki* chronicles.

## Foundation: Empress Genmei and the Capital Move

The Nara period opened on **April 13, 710**, when **Empress Genmei** (r. 707–715) formally transferred the imperial court from Fujiwara-kyō to the newly constructed Heijō-kyō, following a Chinese geomantic layout. The decision to build a permanent capital was itself a significant ideological statement: unlike earlier courts, which relocated upon each emperor's death according to ritual pollution taboos, Heijō-kyō was conceived as an enduring seat of power—a symbol of Japanese dynastic continuity modeled on Tang China's Chang'an.

The city was laid out on a rectangular grid of roughly 5.3 kilometers (north–south) by 4.7 kilometers (east–west), divided into eastern (*Sakyo*) and western (*Ukyo*) sectors by the central Suzaku Avenue, a grand processional boulevard 75 meters wide. At its height, the capital housed an estimated **200,000 residents**, making it one of the largest cities in the 8th-century world. The imperial palace complex (*Daidairi*) occupied the northern center, containing the Great Hall of State (*Daigokuden*), government ministries, and residential quarters.

## Political Structure and Administration

Emperor Mommu (r. 697–707) had already promulgated the **Taihō Code** in **701**, the first complete *ritsuryō* legal compilation, which codified the bureaucratic system Empress Genmei then transferred to the new capital. The code comprised penal law (*ritsu*, 6 volumes) and administrative statutes (*ryō*, 10 volumes), creating a ranked hierarchy of **forty-eight official ranks** (*kurai*), nine imperial ministries (*daijō-kan* and subordinate bodies), and a system of provincial governors (*kokushi*) who administered the roughly sixty provinces into which the realm was divided.

The most powerful figures of the Nara court were often **Fujiwara** family ministers, particularly **Fujiwara no Fuhito** (659–720), whose four sons—Muchimaro, Fusasaki, Umakai, and Maro—simultaneously controlled the Left and Right Ministers' offices and the Ministry of Civil Affairs. This familial concentration established the prototype for the regency system (*sekkan*) that would define the Heian era.

The imperial succession itself remained contested. The reign of Empress **Shōtoku** (r. 765–770, second reign following an earlier reign as Empress Kōken 749–758) and her relationship with the Buddhist priest **Dōkyō** generated alarm that the clergy might seize secular authority. When Empress Shōtoku died in 770 without naming Dōkyō as her successor—a course some at court reportedly proposed—stricter protocols emerged that effectively barred women from the throne for over a millennium, and restricted clerical influence over succession.

## Tang Envoys and International Diplomacy

The Nara court maintained active diplomatic and cultural exchange with Tang China through the **kentōshi** (遣唐使), official embassies dispatched roughly every twenty years. These missions typically comprised **500–600 members**—scholars, monks, artisans, musicians—who traveled aboard four ships to study Tang institutions, collect texts, and observe Chinese administrative practice at first hand. Key embassies departed in 702, 717, 733, 752, and 777. Scholars returning from these missions brought Buddhist scriptures, Confucian texts, Chinese music (*gagaku*), medical knowledge, and artisanal techniques that transformed Nara culture.

Among the most significant returnees was **Genbō**, a monk who brought back 5,000 volumes of Buddhist sutras in 736, and **Kibi no Makibi** (695–775), who studied at Chang'an for eighteen years and returned bearing a Tang crossbow, musical instruments, and astrological texts. Makibi eventually rose to the position of Right Minister (*udaijin*), one of the few non-Fujiwara figures to achieve top-level rank in this era.

## Buddhism and Institutional Power

Buddhism achieved unprecedented institutional prominence during Nara, becoming virtually a state religion. The construction of the **Tōdai-ji Temple** (begun 741, consecrated **May 26, 752**) under Emperor Shōmu (r. 724–749) represented the apex of this synthesis. The project mobilized an estimated **2.6 million laborers** over more than a decade. The Great Buddha (*Daibutsu*), formally named *Rushana Butsu* (Vairocana), was cast from approximately **437 tons of bronze**, **130 kg of gold**, and **7 tons of wax**, standing **14.73 meters** tall on a lotus pedestal 3 meters in height—making it the world's largest bronze statue at its completion. The eye-opening ceremony (*kaigen-e*) in 752 drew thousands of monks, diplomats, and nobles, with the Indian monk **Bodhisena** leading the ceremony.

Shōmu also established a **national temple network**: a provincial temple (*kokubunji*) and provincial nunnery (*kokubunniji*) in each of Japan's sixty-odd provinces, creating roughly 120 institutions that served simultaneously as centers of literacy, manuscript production, state ritual, and social welfare. The *Tōdai-ji* functioned as the head temple for all provincial temples nationwide.

The **six Nara Buddhist schools** (*Nanto rokushū*)—Kusha, Jōjitsu, Ritsu, Sanron, Hossō, and Kegon—represented the Japanese reception of Chinese doctrinal traditions. Unlike the Heian Tendai and Shingon schools (which emphasized salvific practice accessible to laypeople), these Nara schools focused primarily on monastic discipline and scholastic study. The **Ritsu** school, centered on vinaya ordination rules, was anchored at **Tōshōdai-ji** temple, founded in 759 by the Chinese monk **Ganjin** (Jianzhen), who had undertaken five failed sea crossings over twelve years and lost his sight in the effort before finally reaching Japan in 753.

## Literary and Intellectual Achievements

### The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki

The Nara period witnessed the compilation of Japan's oldest written records. The **Kojiki** (古事記, "Records of Ancient Matters"), completed in **712** under the editorial direction of **Ō no Yasumaro**, is the oldest surviving Japanese chronicle, recording mythological origins—the creation of the islands by the deities Izanagi and Izanami, the birth of the sun goddess Amaterasu, and the descent of the imperial line—as well as early historical narratives. The text employed a mixed script of Chinese characters used both for their meaning and phonetically (*man'yōgana*), reflecting the challenge of rendering Japanese speech in a writing system designed for an unrelated language.

The **Nihon Shoki** (日本書紀, "Chronicles of Japan"), completed in **720** and presented to Empress Genshō's court, provided a more formal annalistic account modeled on Tang dynastic histories. Written entirely in classical Chinese, it covered events from the age of the gods through the reign of Empress Jitō (r. 686–697) and presented Japan as a legitimate East Asian polity comparable to Tang and Silla. Together, these texts established Japan's historical self-understanding and its place within the Sinocentric regional order.

### The Man'yōshū

The **Man'yōshū** (万葉集, "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves"), compiled in the late 8th century under the editorship of **Ōtomo no Yakamochi** (718–785), contains approximately **4,516 poems** in 20 volumes, spanning from the mid-7th century through 759. It is Japan's oldest surviving poetry anthology and one of the world's great literary monuments. The collection captures voices from across social strata—emperors, provincial officials, soldiers stationed on frontier garrisons (*sakimori*), and anonymous commoners—in the Japanese poetic forms of *chōka* (long poem) and *tanka* (short poem of 5-7-5-7-7 syllables).

Notable poets include **Kakinomoto no Hitomaro** (fl. late 7th century), whose elegy for Prince Takechi (689) and poems on parting and longing are considered among the Japanese language's highest literary achievements; **Yamabe no Akahito** (fl. early 8th century), celebrated for landscape poetry including a famous verse on Mount Fuji; and **Lady Ōtomo no Sakanoue**, one of the anthology's major female contributors.

## Architecture and the Arts

Nara-period architecture, represented by surviving structures at **Hōryū-ji**, **Tōdai-ji**, and **Tōshōdai-ji**, constitutes the oldest extant wooden architectural ensemble in the world. The **Hōryū-ji** complex in Ikaruga (completed by early 7th century, rebuilt after 670 fire), though predating the Nara period, exemplifies the Tang-influenced style that characterized Nara construction: hip-and-gable roofing, bracketed eaves, vermilion columns, and white plaster walls arranged around open courtyards.

Buddhist sculpture flourished under imperial patronage. Major works include the **Shōsō-in** repository at Tōdai-ji (dedicated 756), which contains over **9,000 objects** donated to the temple by Empress Kōmyō following Emperor Shōmu's death—lacquerware, Persian glass vessels, Central Asian textiles, musical instruments, and documents—preserving an extraordinary record of 8th-century international exchange.

## Economic Development and Daily Life

The *ritsuryō* legal code theoretically allocated rice paddies to every household through a land redistribution system called the **handen shuju hō** (班田授受法): adult males received 2 *tan* (approximately 0.24 hectares), women two-thirds that amount, and slaves half a male allotment, with plots to be reassigned every six years. In practice, this system broke down rapidly. From as early as **723**, the imperial court issued the *Sanze isshin no hō* (三世一身法), permitting newly reclaimed land to be retained privately for three generations. The **743 Konden Einen Shizai Hō** (Edict on Permanent Private Ownership of Newly Reclaimed Land) went further, allowing permanent private ownership of all newly cultivated fields—a policy that accelerated the growth of private temple and aristocratic estates (*shōen*) and undermined the state's tax base.

The capital sustained its large population through rice taxes (*so*), textile levies (*chō*), and corvée labor (*yō*). Provincial grain was transported to the capital along a national road network (*gokishichidō*) with relay stations (*ekiba*) at regular intervals. Specialized craft production supplied the court: weavers at the Bureau of Weaving (*Oribeno-tsukasa*) produced silks and brocades, potters at kiln sites supplied gray *sueki* ceramics and three-color Tang-style *sansai* glazed ware, and metalworkers produced bronze mirrors, bells, and ritual implements.

## Decline and Transition

By the late 8th century, the Nara political system began to fragment under multiple pressures: the accumulated economic power of the great temples, the Fujiwara's tightening grip on court appointments, epidemic disease (smallpox outbreaks in **735–737** killed an estimated **25–35% of the population**, including Fujiwara no Fuhito's four sons), and the strain of sustaining an elaborate capital bureaucracy from an eroding land-tax base.

Emperor Kanmu (r. 781–806), seeking to escape the institutional power of the Nara temples and rebuild imperial authority, relocated the capital first to **Nagaoka-kyō** in **784** (a project abandoned after the assassination of its chief planner, Fujiwara no Tanetsugu, in 785), then definitively to **Heian-kyō** (modern Kyoto) in **794**. This shift northward marked not only a physical relocation but a strategic reorientation: the new capital was positioned to balance aristocratic factions and was designed to limit the influence of established Nara temple complexes, whose abbots had wielded disproportionate secular power throughout the preceding century.

## Cultural Legacy

The Nara period established foundational elements of Japanese civilization: a written literary tradition grounded in Chinese characters and phonetic notation, a bureaucratic governance model adapted from Tang China, a syncretic approach to Buddhism and native *kami* veneration (*shinbutsu-shūgō*) that would persist until the forced separation of 1868, and the intellectual and artistic forms that matured during the Heian period. The temples and statuary of Nara—including Tōdai-ji, Hōryū-ji, Kasuga Grand Shrine, and surrounding parkland—were collectively inscribed as **UNESCO World Heritage Sites in 1998**, tangible links to a formative epoch that witnessed Japan's emergence as a literate, organized East Asian state.
