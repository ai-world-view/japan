# Russo-Japanese War

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō, General Oyama Iwao, Tsar Nicholas II, General Aleksei Kuropatkin, President Theodore Roosevelt (mediator)

## Summary

The Russo-Japanese War (February 8, 1904 – September 5, 1905) was a pivotal conflict between the Russian Empire and the Japanese Empire, fought primarily over competing imperial interests in Manchuria and Korea. Japan opened hostilities with a surprise torpedo attack on the Russian Pacific Squadron anchored at Port Arthur (Lüshunkou) on the night of February 8–9, 1904, before a formal declaration of war was transmitted to St. Petersburg—a tactic Japan would repeat, with larger consequences, at Pearl Harbor in 1941. The war lasted nineteen months and encompassed a prolonged siege of Port Arthur (August 1904 – January 2, 1905), the massive land battle of Mukden (February–March 1905), and the annihilating naval engagement at Tsushima Strait (May 27–28, 1905). The Treaty of Portsmouth, signed on September 5, 1905, at the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard in Kittery, Maine—mediated by U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt, who was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1906 for his role—formally ended the conflict with a decisive, though costly, Japanese victory.

## Background and Causes

### Competing Imperialisms in Northeast Asia

By 1900 both Russia and Japan had recognized Manchuria and Korea as strategic prizes. Russia had extracted from China the right to build the Chinese Eastern Railway (1896) across northern Manchuria to Vladivostok, and in 1898 leased Port Arthur as a warm-water Pacific naval base. When Russia refused to evacuate Manchuria after the 1900 Boxer Rebellion suppression, and then maneuvered to extend its influence into northern Korea, Japanese leaders concluded that negotiations had failed. Japan's strategic calculation was stark: acting sooner, while its newly modernized Meiji military was at peak readiness and Russia's Trans-Siberian Railway was not yet complete (the single-track line could carry only limited reinforcements), offered better odds than waiting.

### Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)

The 1902 Anglo-Japanese Alliance provided Japan diplomatic cover and deterred France or Germany from intervening on Russia's side (as had happened in the Triple Intervention of 1895, which stripped Japan of the Liaodong Peninsula after the First Sino-Japanese War). Without this guarantee, Japan's war gamble would have been far riskier.

## Major Campaigns

### Attack on Port Arthur (February 8–9, 1904)

Vice Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō dispatched ten destroyers to attack the Russian anchorage at Port Arthur during the night of February 8–9 without warning. The torpedo attack damaged two battleships and a cruiser but failed to deliver a knock-out blow. The subsequent daylight engagement on February 9 was inconclusive, but the attack neutralized the Russian Pacific Squadron's offensive capability and established Japanese naval superiority in Yellow Sea approaches.

### Battle of the Yellow Sea (August 10, 1904)

After months of blockade and siege, the Russian Pacific Squadron attempted to break out toward Vladivostok under Rear Admiral Vilgelm Vitgeft on August 10. In the resulting engagement, Vitgeft was killed by a shell on his flagship *Tsesarevich*; the Russian fleet scattered, with ships interned in neutral ports (Qingdao, Saigon, Manila) rather than reaching Vladivostok. The battle eliminated the Russian Pacific Squadron as a fighting force.

### Siege of Port Arthur (August 1904 – January 2, 1905)

The land siege of Port Arthur was among the war's most costly engagements. Japanese Third Army under General Nogi Maresuke launched frontal assaults on the fortified Russian positions, suffering catastrophic losses—by one estimate, over 57,000 Japanese casualties at Port Arthur alone. The capture of 203 Metre Hill (November 1904) allowed Japanese artillery to bombard and sink the remaining Russian warships in the harbor. Port Arthur surrendered January 2, 1905; General Anatoly Stoessel's Russian garrison had been reduced from roughly 40,000 men to fewer than 24,000 effectives.

### Battle of Mukden (February 20 – March 10, 1905)

The largest land battle of the war pitted approximately 330,000 Japanese troops (Field Marshal Oyama Iwao commanding) against roughly 340,000 Russians under General Kuropatkin across a 70-kilometer front near Mukden (present-day Shenyang, China). After three weeks of fighting, Kuropatkin ordered a retreat northward, losing approximately 89,000 casualties (killed, wounded, captured) against Japanese losses of around 75,000. The battle was the largest land engagement since Sedan (1870), and its scale foreshadowed the industrialized warfare of World War I.

### Battle of Tsushima (May 27–28, 1905)

Russia's desperate response was to dispatch the Baltic Fleet—renamed the Second Pacific Squadron under Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky—on an 18,000-nautical-mile voyage around Africa and through the Indian Ocean to reinforce its position. The fleet arrived exhausted and its crews demoralized after a voyage lasting from October 1904 to May 1905. Admiral Tōgō intercepted the Russian fleet in Tsushima Strait between Korea and Japan on May 27. Using the tactical maneuver of "crossing the T," Tōgō's fleet annihilated the Russian force: of 38 Russian ships, 21 were sunk, 7 captured, and 6 interned; Japan lost 3 torpedo boats. Russian casualties exceeded 5,000 dead and 6,000 captured; Japanese, fewer than 700. Tsushima remains the most one-sided naval defeat in modern history and ensured Japan's strategic victory.

## Human and Material Cost

The war's total casualties were severe for both sides:
- **Japan**: approximately 88,000 military dead, 153,000 wounded; war expenditure of roughly ¥1.73 billion, nearly twice the prewar national budget, financed through foreign bond issues arranged by banker Jacob Schiff in London and New York.
- **Russia**: approximately 52,000 military dead, 146,000 wounded or sick, 74,000 captured; enormous naval matériel losses.

Japan's victory came at economic exhaustion. When Roosevelt's mediation produced a peace without a Russian indemnity payment, Japanese public opinion exploded in the Hibiya Riot (September 5–7, 1905), in which crowds torched police boxes and churches, leaving seventeen dead. The popular expectation of a large war indemnity—as Japan had received from China in 1895—had not been met, and the government faced lasting domestic resentment.

## Treaty of Portsmouth (September 5, 1905)

Under U.S. mediation, Russia ceded to Japan:
- The southern half of Sakhalin Island (south of 50°N latitude), designated Karafuto Prefecture
- The lease of the Liaodong Peninsula, including Port Arthur and Dalian (Dairen)
- The southern branch of the Chinese Eastern Railway from Changchun to Port Arthur (reorganized as the South Manchuria Railway)
- Recognition of Japan's "paramount political, military, and economic interests" in Korea

Russia retained northern Manchuria and its railway interests there, a concession Japan accepted partly because its own finances were nearly exhausted.

## Consequences

### Japan's Rise as a Great Power

The war confirmed Japan as the dominant power in East Asia and a recognized member of the international great-power system. Korea became a Japanese protectorate in November 1905 (the Eulsa Treaty, imposed after the war) and was formally annexed in 1910. Manchuria became a zone of Japanese economic and military penetration that would expand into full occupation by 1931. Japan's victory also generated enormous national confidence, feeding a strain of expansionist nationalism that the Taisho-era liberal interlude (1912–1926) only partially checked.

### The 1905 Russian Revolution

News of the Bloody Sunday massacre (January 22, 1905) and continuous military defeats fueled revolution inside Russia. Mutinies, labor strikes, and the *Potemkin* naval mutiny (June 1905) forced Tsar Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, creating Russia's first parliament (the Duma). Though the revolution was suppressed, its dynamics—and the humiliation of 1905—directly fed the conditions that produced the February and October Revolutions of 1917.

### Global Significance

Japan's victory shattered the European assumption that non-Western nations were inherently inferior in modern military capacity. The news electrified anti-colonial movements in Asia and the Middle East: the Ottoman Empire, Persia, India, and China drew lessons from a non-Christian, non-European nation's defeat of a European great power. For Japan itself, the war's outcome became an ideological touchstone, cited by militarists in the 1930s to justify further imperial expansion, even as the actual human and financial costs of 1905 illustrated the limits of that expansion.
