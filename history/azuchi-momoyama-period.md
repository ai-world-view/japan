# Azuchi-Momoyama Period

**Category**: History  
**Key figures**: Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Tokugawa Ieyasu, Sen no Rikyū, Luis Frois

## Summary

The Azuchi-Momoyama period (1568–1603) represents the brief but transformative era of Japan's political unification and cultural efflorescence, bridging the chaos of the Sengoku wars and the order of the Edo shogunate. Named for the two great castles—Nobunaga's Azuchi Castle on Lake Biwa (1576–1582) and Hideyoshi's Momoyama Castle near Kyoto (1593–1615)—this era witnessed the consolidation of Japan under three successive military leaders: Oda Nobunaga, who broke the power of the feudal daimyo and the Buddhist temples; Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who completed unification and attempted foreign conquest; and Tokugawa Ieyasu, whose victory at Sekigahara (1600) inaugurated the Tokugawa shogunate and 264 years of peace.

The period was marked by extraordinary cultural patronage and artistic innovation. Both Nobunaga and Hideyoshi were prolific collectors and patrons of the tea ceremony, painting, poetry, and drama. The *kinran-de* style of gilt-leather and gold-leaf craftsmanship flourished; the *Kanō* school of painting dominated court commissions; and the construction of massive castles with revolutionary new fortification designs—featuring high stone walls, angular bastions, and inner citadels—reflected both military innovation and political ambition. Hideyoshi's grand Osaka Castle (1585) exemplified this architectural revolution.

Foreign contact, particularly with Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries arriving from c. 1543 onward, enriched the period's cultural exchange. Nobunaga and Hideyoshi welcomed missionaries and Western technology (especially firearms, which had already transformed Sengoku warfare after 1543), though Hideyoshi's later Bateren Expulsion Edict of 1587 initiated a suppression that would culminate in the Tokugawa *sakoku* isolation policy. The two invasions of Korea (1592–1598) under Hideyoshi, though ultimately failures, mobilized vast resources and profoundly affected East Asian geopolitics.

## Oda Nobunaga (1534–1582): The First Unifier

Oda Nobunaga was born on June 23, 1534, into the minor Oda clan of Owari Province (modern Aichi Prefecture). Known in his youth as the "Fool of Owari" (*Owari no Outsuke*) for his unconventional behavior, he rapidly proved himself a military innovator of the first order. After consolidating control over Owari by 1560, he scored a spectacular upset at the Battle of Okehazama (June 12, 1560), killing the powerful rival warlord Imagawa Yoshimoto despite being vastly outnumbered—a victory that announced him as a major force in Sengoku politics.

Nobunaga's most decisive military innovation was the systematic deployment of firearms (*tanegashima*, the arquebuses introduced by the Portuguese in 1543). At the Battle of Nagashino (June 28, 1575), he deployed approximately 3,000 arquebusiers in rotating volley fire behind wooden palisades, devastating the cavalry charges of Takeda Katsuyori's once-feared mounted samurai. This battle is widely cited as a watershed moment in Japanese military history, demonstrating that disciplined infantry firepower could overcome traditional cavalry tactics.

Politically, Nobunaga was ruthless in breaking the power of institutions that opposed unification. He destroyed the Enryaku-ji temple complex on Mount Hiei—the great Tendai Buddhist headquarters near Kyoto—in September 1571, burning its thousands of buildings and massacring its monks and lay population to eliminate a formidable political and military threat. He similarly prosecuted a ten-year siege of the Ikkō-ikki peasant-Buddhist stronghold of Ishiyama Honganji in Osaka (1570–1580) and systematically suppressed other armed Buddhist communities.

On the night of June 21, 1582—the *Honnō-ji Incident*—Nobunaga was betrayed and forced to commit suicide at the Honnō-ji temple in Kyoto by one of his most trusted generals, Akechi Mitsuhide, in an act of treachery whose ultimate motivation remains debated by historians. He had conquered approximately 30 of Japan's 68 provinces at the time of his death. Within thirteen days, Toyotomi Hideyoshi had defeated and killed Mitsuhide at the Battle of Yamazaki, positioning himself as Nobunaga's legitimate heir.

## Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598): The Unifier

Toyotomi Hideyoshi remains one of history's most extraordinary social ascents: born in 1537 to a peasant family in Nakamura, Owari Province (modern Nagoya), he rose to become the de facto ruler of all Japan by 1590—the only person in Japanese history to achieve this without samurai ancestry. Initially serving Nobunaga as a lowly sandal-bearer (*zōri-tori*), his intelligence, administrative skill, and military talent brought him rapid promotion.

Following the Honnō-ji Incident, Hideyoshi secured his position as Nobunaga's successor through a combination of military prowess and political maneuvering. By 1585 he had received the court title of *kampaku* (imperial regent) and in 1586 was granted the surname "Toyotomi" by Emperor Ōgimachi—titles that gave his rule imperial legitimacy despite his non-aristocratic origins. After defeating the powerful Shimazu clan of Kyushu in 1587 and forcing the submission of the Hōjō clan with the siege of Odawara Castle in 1590, Hideyoshi achieved the complete unification of Japan.

Hideyoshi's administrative innovations were as significant as his military achievements. His *Taikō kenchi* (land survey, conducted 1582–1598) established standardized land measurements and tax assessments across Japan, replacing the fragmented feudal land system. His Sword Hunt (*katanagari*) edict of 1588 confiscated weapons from non-samurai, formally separating the social classes of warrior (*shi*), farmer (*nō*), artisan (*kō*), and merchant (*shō*)—a rigid status hierarchy that would define Tokugawa society for the next two and a half centuries.

Hideyoshi's two invasions of Korea—the Bunroku Campaign (1592–1593) and the Keichō Campaign (1597–1598)—were ultimately catastrophic failures. The first campaign saw Japanese forces advance rapidly through Korea to capture Seoul (May 1592) and Pyongyang (June 1592), but Korean naval resistance under Admiral Yi Sun-sin (whose famous "turtle ships" disrupted Japanese supply lines) and a Ming dynasty Chinese intervention forced a withdrawal. Hideyoshi died on September 18, 1598, and his forces were immediately recalled from Korea.

## Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616): The Patient Strategist

Tokugawa Ieyasu was born on January 31, 1543, in Okazaki, Mikawa Province (modern Aichi Prefecture), into the minor Matsudaira clan. Held as a political hostage for much of his childhood—first by the Oda clan and later by the Imagawa—he spent years developing the patience and political acumen that would define his career. He served both Nobunaga (as an ally from 1562) and Hideyoshi (as a subordinate from 1584) while carefully building his power base in the eight Kanto provinces.

Following Hideyoshi's death in 1598, Ieyasu was one of five regents (*go-tairō*) appointed to govern on behalf of Hideyoshi's infant son Toyotomi Hideyori. Using his position to build alliances, Ieyasu provoked a decisive confrontation at the Battle of Sekigahara (October 21, 1600) in Mino Province (modern Gifu Prefecture), where his eastern coalition defeated the western coalition supporting the Toyotomi cause. The battle involved approximately 160,000 combatants on both sides and lasted a single day, decided in part by the defection of Kobayakawa Hideaki and other western generals to Ieyasu's side. In 1603, Emperor Go-Yōzei conferred on him the title of *sei-i taishōgun*—formally inaugurating the Tokugawa shogunate.

Ieyasu eliminated the last Toyotomi resistance in the Sieges of Osaka Castle (1614–1615), forcing Hideyori's suicide, and died at Sunpu (modern Shizuoka) on June 1, 1616. He was posthumously deified as Tōshō Daigongen and enshrined at the magnificent Nikkō Tōshō-gū mausoleum (completed 1636).

## Castle Architecture

The Azuchi-Momoyama period revolutionized Japanese castle design in response to the widespread use of firearms. Medieval castles had typically been simple wooden palisades on hilltops; the new castles of the unification era featured massive stone bases (*ishigaki*) with steeply angled walls that deflected projectiles, multi-story tower keeps (*tenshukaku*) visible for miles as symbols of political power, and networks of moats, gates, and inner baileys designed for systematic defense in depth.

**Azuchi Castle** (built 1576, destroyed 1582): Constructed on a promontory above Lake Biwa in Ōmi Province (modern Shiga Prefecture), Azuchi was the most revolutionary castle of its era—a seven-story tower keep, estimated at 46 meters tall, decorated throughout with gold leaf, Kanō school paintings, and lacquered interiors. The Jesuit missionary Luis Frois, who visited in 1582, described it as exceeding in beauty any palace in Europe. It was destroyed in the confusion following the Honnō-ji Incident, its full scale now known only from contemporary accounts and surviving planning documents.

**Osaka Castle** (begun 1583): Hideyoshi's greatest architectural project, Osaka Castle was constructed on the site of the former Ishiyama Honganji fortress and was the largest castle in Japan at completion. Its stone base and outer moat system remain today, though the current superstructure dates to a 1931 concrete reconstruction.

**Himeji Castle**: Substantially expanded by Hideyoshi in 1581 and further developed by Ikeda Terumasa after 1601, Himeji is the finest surviving example of Azuchi-Momoyama castle architecture. Its gleaming white plastered walls, complex defensive labyrinth, and elegant multi-tiered silhouette have earned it designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1993) and the informal title of "White Egret Castle" (*Shirasagi-jō*).

## Arts and Culture

The Azuchi-Momoyama period produced an artistic culture of extraordinary richness, characterized by bold colors, lavish gold leaf, and monumental scale—a deliberate contrast to the subtle ink-wash aesthetics of Muromachi Zen culture.

**The Kanō School**: The dominant painting school of the era, reaching its apex under Kanō Eitoku (1543–1590), who served as Nobunaga's principal court painter. Eitoku's monumental folding screen paintings (*byōbu-e*) for Azuchi Castle and Osaka Castle featured sweeping compositions of pine trees, eagles, and mythic landscapes in vivid mineral pigments on gold-leaf grounds. His grandson Kanō Tan'yū (1602–1674) would carry the school's dominance into the early Edo period.

**Sen no Rikyū and the Tea Ceremony**: The tea ceremony (*chanoyu*) reached its aesthetic apex through Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591), who served as tea master to both Nobunaga and Hideyoshi. Rikyū perfected the aesthetic of *wabi-cha* (rustic tea), emphasizing simplicity, natural materials, and spiritual depth over opulence. His designs for the tea house—including the famous two-mat *Taian* tea room at Myōki-an temple in Yamazaki (c. 1582, now a National Treasure)—established the architectural standard for tea ceremony spaces. In 1591, Hideyoshi ordered Rikyū's suicide over disputed artistic and political tensions, making him a martyr figure in the history of Japanese aesthetics. See [Tea Ceremony](../culture-society/tea-ceremony.md) for a fuller account.

**Noh and Kyōgen Drama**: Both Nobunaga and Hideyoshi were enthusiastic patrons of Noh drama, with Hideyoshi himself learning to perform Noh pieces and commissioning plays about his own life. The era saw Noh spread beyond its court origins into the merchant and warrior classes, laying groundwork for the popular dramatic arts of the subsequent Edo period.

**Nanban ("Southern Barbarian") Art**: The term *nanban* was applied to Portuguese and Spanish traders and missionaries, and *nanban bijutsu* (nanban art) refers to the vibrant tradition of depicting Western elements—European dress, ships, globes, firearms, playing cards—in Japanese screens, lacquerware, and textiles. These works, produced primarily for elite patrons and the export market, represent one of the earliest syntheses of Japanese and European aesthetic traditions.

## Foreign Contact and Christianity

Portuguese traders first reached Japan in 1543, landing on the island of Tanegashima in Kyushu, and introduced firearms that would transform Japanese warfare within a generation. Jesuit missionaries, led by Francisco Xavier (1506–1552), arrived in 1549 and within decades had attracted an estimated 150,000–300,000 Christian converts, concentrated in Kyushu. Both Nobunaga—who found the Jesuits politically useful as counterweights to the Buddhist temples—and the early Hideyoshi tolerated and at times actively supported Christian missions.

Hideyoshi's attitude shifted in 1587, when he issued the Bateren Expulsion Edict ordering Jesuit missionaries to leave Japan within twenty days, though enforcement was initially lax. The 1597 crucifixion of the Twenty-Six Martyrs of Japan in Nagasaki—including six European missionaries and twenty Japanese converts—signaled a decisive hardening of policy. Under Tokugawa rule, the suppression intensified: all Christian practice was banned by 1613, the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638 (a peasant and Christian uprising on the Shimabara Peninsula, Kyushu) was crushed with the loss of an estimated 37,000 rebel lives, and Japan's subsequent *sakoku* (closed country) policy restricted foreign contact to the Dutch trading post on Dejima Island in Nagasaki harbor and limited trade with China.

## Significance

The Azuchi-Momoyama period represents Japan's transition from feudal fragmentation to centralized political authority. The three unifiers—Nobunaga's military innovations, Hideyoshi's administrative genius, and Ieyasu's patience and strategic acumen—created the institutional and cultural foundations of the early-modern Japanese state. The period's castle architecture, artistic patronage, and political consolidation established a model of unified authority that would endure through the Edo period and into the modern era. The foreign contact and eventual *sakoku* isolation shaped Japan's unique cultural trajectory, allowing a distinctive synthesis that later propelled Japan's rapid Meiji-era modernization. The cultural florescence of this era—in tea ceremony aesthetics, painting schools, ceramic traditions, and dramatic arts—remains foundational to Japanese aesthetics and continues to define Japanese cultural identity globally.
